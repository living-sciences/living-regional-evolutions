## Summary

This paper characterizes how U.S. states adjust to shocks over the postwar period and builds a compact model to interpret the pattern. The central empirical contrast is between employment, which behaves like a unit-root process so that shocks leave permanent level effects, and relative unemployment and wages, which revert to state-specific means within roughly five to ten years. From this the authors draw their headline mechanism: after an adverse labor-demand shock a state returns to normal mainly because workers leave, not because wages fall enough to pull jobs in. The trivariate VAR quantifies this cleanly, attributing about two-thirds of the first-year employment adjustment to net out-migration, and the wage, house-price, and consumer-price systems reinforce it by showing that real consumption wages barely move because housing prices absorb much of the local shock. The conceptual move that ties the facts together is a two-margin model in which both workers and firms are mobile: fixed amenity differences ($x_{si}$ for workers, $x_{di}$ for firms) generate permanent growth-rate differences, while mobility keeps relative wages and unemployment stationary. The framing toward European monetary union gives the descriptive work unusual reach.

The evidence is largely descriptive and rests on a strong identifying assumption, that unforecastable employment movements are labor-demand shocks; the paper defends this with cross-sectional and observable-shock checks that are suggestive but not decisive. Reconstruction on modern public data confirms the qualitative backbone, especially the permanence of employment shocks and the migration-dominated adjustment, while much of the exact magnitude structure could not be independently verified because the key input series are archival or privately supplied. Two completed follow-up studies re-estimate the model through 2025-26 and find the mechanism intact but the migration channel materially weaker. Overall this is a landmark descriptive contribution whose main claims survive scrutiny, with local inconsistencies in how persistence and elasticities are described.

## Strengths

- **Permanent employment shocks against transitory unemployment shocks.** The paper's organizing fact, that relative employment carries a unit root while relative unemployment mean-reverts within about a decade, is stated precisely and reproduces on public data: a rebuilt pooled AR for relative employment yields a long-run response near 1.5 (paper: about 1.5) and the relative-unemployment response is essentially zero by ten years.
- **Public-data regressions reproduce almost exactly.** The Figure 1 persistence regression (slope 0.698, R^2 0.754 versus 0.70/0.75; ex-Arizona/Florida/Nevada R^2 0.610 versus 0.60) and the personal-income convergence regression (slope about -0.012, |t| about 7.1, R^2 0.52 versus -0.01/6.8/0.50) come out within a few percent of the printed values.
- **Migration channel quantified, not just asserted.** Converting the impulse responses into worker counts, the paper shows about 0.65 of the first-year adjustment runs through net out-migration, and the reconstruction lands near 0.60; this is the paper's most consequential and most checkable result.
- **Tentative results are flagged as tentative.** The authors repeatedly mark the price-level and consumption-wage work as more provisional because the price indexes are metro-level proxies for state prices, which is the appropriate posture given the data.

## Weaknesses

- **The observable-shock check does not cleanly support the identifying assumption.** In the concluding validation, the industry-mix instrument moves the Okun coefficient in the predicted direction but the defense-contract instrument moves it the other way, and both IV estimates are "substantially less precise." Reading all three approaches as jointly supportive overstates evidence that is mixed and imprecise, and this bears directly on the paper's core claim that employment innovations are demand shocks.
- **Persistence language is inconsistent with the paper's own numbers.** The summary calls relative-wage shocks "transitory, disappearing within a decade or so," but the univariate wage response is 0.57 at ten years and 0.19 at twenty; separately, unemployment persistence is described as "one-half decade or so" in the introduction and "six to ten years" in the body. A reader taking the summary at face value will misjudge how persistent wages are and how the two variables differ.
- **The unit elasticity of wages to unemployment mixes horizons.** The "approximately unity" figure divides a wage trough at about six years by an unemployment peak at about two years, so it is not the elasticity at any single horizon and should not be read as a contemporaneous relationship.
- **The mix instrument's exclusion restriction is asserted too strongly.** The claim that no two-digit sector is concentrated in a state is "clearly satisfied" ignores clear counterexamples (autos in Michigan, aerospace in Washington, oil and gas in Texas and Oklahoma) where a state carries much of the national sector, which is exactly the case that would break the instrument.
- **The metro price systems are driven by state, not metro, employment.** The house-price and CPI systems attach a state-level employment innovation to metro-level prices; if the two employment series diverge, the estimated price responses that underpin the "consumption wages barely move" conclusion are attenuated.
- **Minor internal discrepancies.** The average employment rate appears as both 0.936 and 0.932; u_it is redefined mid-paper from a national-relative rate to a level unemployment-to-employment ratio; and the Okun-coefficient definition ("the negative of the ratio") has the opposite sign to the reported negative values.

## Reproducibility & code

The original repository shipped empty, so the pipeline reconstructed the analysis from the manuscript using modern public series (LAUS, FRED). Weighted replication score 0.30 (32 of 35 claims counted): 3 claims matched, 12 were partial, 8 did not match, and the remainder were blocked for lack of the original data, so the low score reflects data unavailability and reconstruction proxies far more than refuted claims.

- **The public-data backbone reproduces.** The Figure 1 persistence and income-convergence regressions, the ADF unit-root pattern for wages (3 states reject), the permanent employment hump, and the near-zero-by-ten-years unemployment response all reproduced closely. The five-year unemployment retention came out near 0.08 rather than the paper's 0.29 (the rebuilt series reverts even faster), so the low-persistence conclusion holds while that specific magnitude does not.
- **The migration decomposition holds qualitatively but not numerically.** The rebuilt trivariate VAR reproduces migration-dominated adjustment (about 0.60 of the first-year response versus 0.65) but not the year-1 unemployment (about 0.20 pp versus 0.32 pp) and participation (about 0.49 pp versus 0.17 pp) responses or the pooled Okun coefficient, in part because unemployment and participation had to be proxied from ratios.
- **Figure 3 and the pre-1976 unemployment work cannot be rebuilt.** Public state unemployment begins in 1976, and the paper's earlier rates rest on a privately supplied LMA-based series, so the 1975/1985 and 1970/1990 correlations are not independently checkable.
- **Three privately supplied or proprietary inputs block major sections.** The industry- and worker-adjusted CPS wage series is only "available upon request"; the metro house-price series was supplied privately (NAR, via Poterba); and both the defense-contract and Bartik industry-mix instruments were privately supplied. As a result the adjusted-wage responses, the consumption-wage conclusion, and the entire observable-shock validation could not be reproduced.

## Follow-up studies

Two completed follow-up studies extend the analysis with new data and both confirm the mechanism while showing it has weakened. The living-update study re-estimates through 2025 and finds relative-employment shocks still permanent (year-20 response about 1.52) and the unemployment half-life still 3-4 years, but the first-year out-migration share of adjustment has fallen from 0.38 (1978-90) to about 0.28 (2011-25), and the cross-generation growth-persistence slope has dropped from 0.70 to 0.37. The theory-update study recalibrates the model to 2026 and estimates that the structural migration elasticity to relative unemployment has roughly halved (about 0.0047 in 1976-90 to about 0.0022 recently, an out-of-sample check confirming this is not a window artifact), with the implication that severe or persistent local slumps now heal more slowly (half-life about 5 years versus 3). These results sharpen the Weaknesses point about the migration channel: the paper's headline that adjustment runs mainly through people leaving is period-specific, strongest in the sample it was estimated on.

## Recommended Changes

**Essential**

- Reconcile the persistence descriptions with the estimated responses: state separately that unemployment is near zero by ten years while relative wages remain at 0.57 (ten years) and 0.19 (twenty years), and align the "one-half decade" and "six to ten years" statements for unemployment.
- Report the instrumental-variables point estimates and confidence intervals in the observable-shock section, and address why the defense instrument moves the Okun coefficient toward zero, so the identification check is characterized as the mixed evidence it is.
- Release, or document a public-source reconstruction of, the constructed series that block reproduction: the CPS-adjusted state wage series, the pre-1976 state unemployment series, and (where possible) the house-price, defense-contract, and industry-mix inputs.

**Suggested**

- Compute the wage-to-unemployment elasticity at a common horizon rather than as a ratio of two peaks four years apart.
- Add a concentration diagnostic for two-digit sectors (or drop sector-dominant states) to support the mix instrument's exclusion restriction.
- Show robustness of the house-price response to using metro-level rather than state-level employment.
- Fix the small internal inconsistencies: the 0.936/0.932 employment-rate figures, the reuse of u_it for two different quantities, and the sign in the Okun-coefficient definition.

## Future Directions

- **Time-variation in the migration channel.** The follow-up finding that the first-year out-migration share fell from 0.38 to about 0.28 and the migration elasticity roughly halved invites a structural account of rising moving costs and what now substitutes for migration in local adjustment.
- **Who moves and who stays.** The paper flags integrating its aggregate responses with micro-evidence on migrants; linking the state-level impulse responses to individual mobility by age, tenure, and liquidity would test whether liquidity-constrained workers drive the out-migration result.
- **Job creation versus destruction.** The authors point to establishment-level job-flow data; decomposing the weak job-creation response into gross creation and destruction would sharpen the claim that firms respond little to local wage declines.
- **Europe with realized data.** The paper's EMU warning was prospective; the same VAR and migration specification can now be estimated on decades of post-euro regional data to test whether adjustment there runs through unemployment rather than migration, as predicted.
